## What the Owner said (his words, condensed)
It cannot be bureaucratic: flexible, reversible, agile, configurable. The point is product value, fast. The harness should question, help and guide the user. Is our T·C·A·D·D principle still valid or too rigid; how does the harness choose what to do, the way a good lead would route work? Scouts and context are key: how is a project handled when it is greenfield versus brownfield? Testing is key but in proportion to the change: a whole feature obviously, a small thing less. If it is frontend, always use Playwright and screenshots. After work, always produce a beautiful minimalist HTML, inspired by diagrams, that lets him understand the architecture that was built. And: how do we get quality and reliability from an autonomous development system, and why is it worth building something different.

## Design questions to answer
1. **TCADD: pipeline or menu?** Decide. Propose the routing model: which signals (size, risk flags, UI or not, known cause, greenfield/brownfield, repo count, confidence of the request) select which steps (interview, scouts, plan, adversarial review, team, verification depth, documentation). Give the full case matrix the router handles, including the ones not mapped today (exploration or spike with no acceptance criterion, docs-only, refactor without behaviour change, dependency upgrade, production incident, UI polish). The route must be decided by the program from declared signals with simple rules, shown with its reason, overridable by the Owner in one command, and reversible in both directions mid-flight without losing work. Say what each step costs when skipped.
2. **Context.** Greenfield versus brownfield: what orq does first in each, what the scouts are asked, what is cached (project card, memory, a code map) so context is not re-bought every slice, and when cached context is stale.
3. **Proportional verification.** Map lane and signals to verification depth, reusing the quality profile (P9). Frontend: Playwright run in the sandbox or outside it (it needs a browser; say which and why), screenshots as evidence in the receipt, what counts as a pass.
4. **The human loop.** How Atena questions and guides instead of taking orders: when to push back, when to propose a cheaper path, how many questions at most per lane. The comprehension report: a single self-contained minimalist HTML per closed slice (and one for the project), with diagrams of what was built and how it connects, screenshots when there is UI, what was verified and what was not; who generates it, from which sources of truth (git diff, receipt, journal), how orq checks it exists and is not invented, and what it costs. Proportional: a fix gets a short one.
5. **Supervision and reliability.** A resident watcher (`orq vigia`): what it observes (panes, heartbeats, stalled workers, budget, RAM), what it may do alone (nudge, pause launches, mark unknown) and what it must escalate; how every loop the harness opens is guaranteed to close; failure modes of an autonomous system from the evidence files and the control for each.
6. **Why different.** One paragraph: the bet versus Superpowers, SwarmForge, pstack and Claude Code agent teams, and what would prove the bet wrong (the measurement that decides).
